Lighthouse Point

Why is the sky blue, and the sunset red?

Both have the same cause. Once you see it, you can't unsee it.

Look up on a clear day and the whole sky glows blue. But space is black, and the sun is white-ish. So where does all that blue come from, and why does it turn orange and red when the sun goes down?

A common guess is that the sky reflects the ocean. It doesn't. The sky is just as blue over deserts. The real answer is stranger and nicer: the air itself is glowing with light it has knocked sideways.

1. Sunlight is every color at once

Sunlight looks white, but it's a mix of all the colors of the rainbow. Isaac Newton showed this with a prism in the 1660s. Each color is light with a different wavelength, meaning the distance between the crests of the wave. Violet and blue have short waves. Red has long ones.

380 nm · violet550 nm · green700 nm · red

A nanometer (nm) is a billionth of a meter. Visible light spans roughly 380 to 700 nm.

2. Air knocks short waves sideways

Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen molecules, and they're tiny: about a thousand times smaller than a wavelength of light. When a light wave passes over a molecule, its electric field shakes the molecule's electrons back and forth. A shaking charge gives off light of its own, sending some of the wave off in a new direction. This is called scattering.

Here's the key fact. Faster shaking radiates far more strongly, and short waves shake electrons faster. Work it through and the strength of scattering goes as 1 / wavelength⁴. Halve the wavelength and you get sixteen times as much scattering. Lord Rayleigh worked this out in 1871, so it's called Rayleigh scattering.

as much scattering as red light (700 nm)

So blue light gets scattered all over the sky, much more than red does. Wherever you look, some of that scattered blue light is heading toward your eye. The blue sky is sunlight that bounced off air.

3. Make your own sky

This is a real (simplified) physics simulation of Earth's atmosphere. It traces light from the sun through miles of air, scatters it off molecules and dust, and works out what reaches your eye. You're standing on the ground, looking toward the sun.

Try this: drag the sun down to the horizon. At sunset, sunlight skims sideways through the atmosphere, crossing almost 40 times as much air as at noon. Along the way, nearly all the blue gets scattered off in other directions before it reaches you. What's left is orange and red, so that's the color of the sun and the clouds it lights. The blue sky and the red sunset are the same effect, seen from different angles.

Then tick the box to make air scatter every color equally. The sky goes white-gray. That's the proof: the blue comes entirely from that 1/λ⁴ preference for short waves.

4. Then why isn't the sky violet?

Violet has an even shorter wavelength than blue, so it scatters even more. Shouldn't the sky be purple? Two things push back against violet. Step through them:

The skylight really does contain more violet than blue. But sunlight has less violet in it to begin with, and our eyes are much less sensitive at the violet end. The sky isn't one wavelength. It's a blend of every color, tilted toward the short end, and that blend is what our eyes read as pale "sky blue".

5. Clouds, haze, and Mars

Clouds are white because water droplets are thousands of times bigger than air molecules. Particles that big scatter all colors about equally. (This is called Mie scattering.) Turn up the "dust & haze" slider above and watch the sky fade toward white. It's the same reason a smoggy or humid sky looks pale.

On Mars, it's backwards. Mars's air is thin, but it's full of fine reddish dust. By day the dust tints the sky a butterscotch color. At sunset, though, the dust scatters blue light forward, close around the sun, so Martian sunsets have a cool blue glow. NASA's Curiosity rover has photographed them.

A loose thread

Here's a twist. After the sun sets, the sky overhead stays a deep blue, but Rayleigh scattering alone can't fully explain that. By then the light overhead has crossed so much air that scattering alone would leave it grayish or yellowish. In 1953 E. O. Hulburt showed the missing piece is ozone, which absorbs some orange and red light (its Chappuis bands). At twilight, the ozone layer quietly paints the sky blue. Even the simple answer to "why is the sky blue" turns out to have a hidden chapter.

You can see this gap in the simulator above. It has no ozone, so when you drag the sun below the horizon, the sky overhead fades to gray instead of deep blue. The model is missing a piece of the real world, and now you know which one.

What's simplified here

  • The simulator tracks just three colors (red, green, blue) instead of the whole spectrum.
  • It counts light scattered once on its way to you, not light that bounces several times. Real skies near the horizon are a bit brighter than shown.
  • It leaves out ozone, clouds, and the shadow of the Earth, and the sun's brightness is fixed, with no eye adjusting to the dark the way yours does at dusk.
  • The bar chart treats sunlight as a 5,778 K blackbody, which is a good approximation of the real solar spectrum but misses its fine detail. Eye response uses an analytic fit to the CIE 1931 color-matching functions (Wyman, Sloan & Shirley, 2013).
