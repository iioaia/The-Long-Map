What is a Fourier transform actually doing?

Any shape you can draw, and any sound you can hear, can be built from circles spinning at steady speeds.

That sounds like a wild claim. In 1807, a French mathematician named Joseph Fourier made it while studying how heat spreads through metal. Some of the greatest mathematicians of his day doubted him. He turned out to be right, and his idea now runs quietly inside almost every piece of technology you own.

The best way to understand it is to watch it happen.

1. A circle makes a wave

Take a circle and spin it at a steady speed. Follow a point on its rim and record only how high it is. Over time, that height traces a smooth, rolling wave called a sine wave. It's the simplest wave there is.

Now for the trick. Put a second, smaller circle on the rim of the first, spinning faster. Then a third on the second, and so on. The point at the very end traces a more complicated wave. Choose the right sizes and speeds and you can build any wave at all, even one with sharp corners.

Try this: start with one circle and slowly add more. With one circle you get a gentle sine wave. Add circles and the square wave's flat tops and steep cliffs appear, as if out of nowhere. Each new circle is smaller and spins faster than the one before, adding finer detail.

This is what a Fourier transform does: it takes something complicated and finds the list of circles (how big, how fast, starting where) that rebuilds it. That list is called the spectrum. The reverse direction, turning circles back into the shape, is the inverse transform.

2. Draw anything

Waves are one thing. But the same trick works for drawings, if the circles move in two directions instead of just up and down. Draw a shape in the box below with your finger or mouse, in one continuous line. The Fourier transform will find the circles, and they'll redraw it for you.

Slide "circles used" all the way down to 1 and you get a plain circle. At 3 or 4 the drawing becomes a rough blob. The biggest circles carry the overall shape. The small ones add the fine detail: corners, squiggles, the bump of a letter. That's the whole secret of compression, as you'll see in section 4. Keep the big circles, throw away the tiny ones, and most people can't tell the difference.

3. Hear it

Sound is a wave of air pressure, so sound can be built from circles too. A musical note is a stack of sine waves: a fundamental (the pitch you hear) plus harmonics at 2, 3, 4… times that frequency. The mix of harmonics is why a flute and a violin playing the same note sound different.

Same pitch the whole time (220 Hz, an A). Only the mix of harmonics changes, and the sound's character changes with it. Your browser builds this tone from exactly these numbers, a literal Fourier series. Keep the volume low at first.

Your ear does a Fourier transform too. Deep inside it, the cochlea is a coiled tube lined with hair cells. Different spots along it respond to different frequencies, so your ear splits incoming sound into its ingredients before your brain ever hears it.

4. You've used this today

Probably dozens of times. Fourier's idea (and its close cousins) is everywhere:

  • Music and podcasts. MP3 and AAC split sound into frequencies, then throw away the ones your ear won't miss.
  • Photos. JPEG does the same thing for images. It breaks small blocks of a picture into patterns of stripes, then drops the finest ones. That's how a photo shrinks to a tenth of its size.
  • Wi-Fi and phones. Modern Wi-Fi and 4G/5G send data on many frequencies at once, and your device uses a fast Fourier transform to pull them apart, millions of times a second.
  • Song recognition. Apps that name a song from a few seconds of audio match the pattern of its strongest frequencies over time.
  • MRI scans. An MRI machine doesn't measure a picture of your body directly. It measures the picture's frequencies, and a Fourier transform turns them into the image doctors see.

All of this depends on computers doing the transform fast. The breakthrough was the Fast Fourier Transform, published by James Cooley and John Tukey in 1965. It turns out Carl Friedrich Gauss had found essentially the same method around 1805, but it sat unnoticed in his notes for over a century.

A loose thread

Go back to the square wave in section 1 and add lots of circles. Look closely at the corners. There's always a little spike that overshoots the flat top. Add more circles and the spike gets narrower, but it never gets shorter. It always overshoots by about 9% of the jump.

In 1898 the physicist Albert Michelson built a brass machine that added up 80 waves. He saw these spikes and assumed his machine was faulty. It wasn't. J. Willard Gibbs explained the spikes in the journal Nature in 1899, and they're now called the Gibbs phenomenon, although Henry Wilbraham had quietly described them 50 years earlier. Circles are perfect at smooth curves and always a little uneasy at sudden jumps. You can sometimes see the same ripples in JPEG photos, as faint halos around sharp edges.

What's simplified here

  • The drawing uses the discrete Fourier transform. It samples your line at 200 evenly spaced points rather than treating it as a perfectly smooth curve.
  • MP3, AAC and JPEG use cosine-based relatives of the Fourier transform (the MDCT and DCT), not the exact one shown here. The idea of splitting into frequencies is the same.
  • Real instruments' harmonics change from moment to moment. The tone above is frozen.
  • The cochlea isn't a perfect frequency analyzer. It's more like a bank of overlapping filters.
